DGC — Three Deal Options
Original proposal plus two structured alternatives · Ash Aziz · DGC Ltd
Option A — Original proposal

£150,000 for 10% of DGC Ltd — ordinary shares

Daniel puts in £150,000. In return he gets 10% of DGC Ltd as ordinary shares — a permanent slice of the company. He owns that 10% for as long as he holds the shares. Each year when DGC pays out profits, he gets 10% of whatever is paid out. If DGC is ever sold, he gets 10% of the sale price. His shares grow in value as DGC grows.

There is no repayment date and no guaranteed return — his income depends entirely on how DGC performs. A shareholders agreement is signed before any money moves, which sets out the rules clearly for everyone.

The cap table after the deal: Ash 45% · Harry 45% · Daniel 10%. All three are owners.

How this deal works — step by step
  1. Daniel writes a cheque for £150,000
    The money goes directly into DGC Ltd as working capital. DGC uses it to stabilise cash flow, fund equipment, or bridge contracts. Daniel receives newly issued ordinary shares — 10% of the total company.
  2. A shareholders agreement is signed first (before any money moves)
    An Isle of Man solicitor drafts a short agreement (approx. £2,000, 2–3 weeks) that sets the rules: how dividends are decided, how Daniel can exit, what decisions require notice to him, and a formula for buying him out. This protects everyone — not just Daniel.
  3. Year 1 — no dividends, business absorbs the capital
    The first year is a grace period. No dividends are paid in the first trading year. The money works in the business. Daniel is a shareholder but receives nothing yet — this is agreed upfront.
  4. From Year 2 onwards — Daniel receives 10% of whatever is paid out
    Each year-end, Ash and Harry review DGC’s accounts and decide how much profit to distribute (target: 40–50%). Daniel automatically receives 10% of that amount. At £5M turnover that’s around £25k/year. At £10M turnover it reaches £50k/year.
  5. Year 5 — Daniel has received his £150k back through dividends alone
    By Year 5 (projecting 10% profit margin), cumulative dividends reach £150,000 — Daniel’s original investment is fully returned just from his share of profits. His shares are still worth £320k. Everything from here is pure upside for him.
  6. From Year 7 onwards — business plateaus, Daniel earns £50k/year in perpetuity
    Once DGC reaches £10M turnover, the model holds flat. Daniel receives £50,000 per year, every year, for as long as he holds his shares. This continues indefinitely — for his lifetime, and potentially his estate’s.
  7. DGC can buy Daniel out from Year 5 onwards
    Ash and Harry have the option (not the obligation) to purchase Daniel’s shares at any time from Year 5 using the formula: 4× annual profit × 10%. At plateau that’s approximately £400,000. Daniel cannot force a buyout — only DGC can trigger this.
Honest pros and cons

WHAT WORKS

  • Cleanest, simplest structure. Daniel owns shares like any shareholder. No ambiguity about what he holds.
  • Daniel has maximum upside — his stake grows in value as DGC grows. If DGC hits £10M he’s sitting on a £400k asset plus ongoing income.
  • Strong alignment: Daniel wants DGC to grow because his shares grow with it. He’s on the same team.
  • IoM 0% corporate tax means every penny of profit flows to shareholders — no tax drag reducing Daniel’s return.
  • SHA protects DGC — no board seat, no veto, no interference. Daniel is a silent partner.

WHAT TO WATCH

  • 10% is permanent. If DGC reaches £10M turnover, that 10% is worth £400k in shares and pays £50k/year forever. That is a very significant long-term cost for a £150k injection today.
  • Daniel’s total return over 17 years (dividends + exit stake) is approximately £1.89M on £150k — a 12.6× return. This is generous given the risk profile of a profitable £3M business.
  • Growth projections are estimates. If DGC doesn’t grow as fast, Daniel’s income is lower — but his 10% stake remains, regardless of performance.
  • Daniel is now permanently on DGC’s cap table. Any future investment rounds, sales, or restructuring must account for his 10%.
  • SHA cost ~£2,000 upfront. Accountant valuation note ~£300. Small but worth noting.

Option B — Ratchet equity

£150,000 for 10% — drops to 5% once Daniel gets his money back

This is the original deal with one important addition: a ratchet clause. Daniel gets 10% of DGC as ordinary shares, exactly as in the original deal. Everything works the same — SHA, dividends, buyback formula. The only difference is a built-in rule: once DGC has paid Daniel back his original £150,000 through dividends (which happens around Year 5), DGC pays Daniel an additional £75,000 to buy back half his shares. He drops from 10% to 5%.

From Year 6 onwards, Daniel holds 5% of DGC permanently — still a meaningful stake, still growing with the business, but DGC has recovered half the equity once the debt has been repaid through profits.

Think of it like this: Phase 1 (Years 1–5) — Daniel at 10%, DGC growing and repaying him. Phase 2 (Year 6 onwards) — Daniel at 5%, smaller slice, much stronger business.

How this deal works — step by step
  1. Exactly the same as the original deal — to begin with
    Daniel invests £150,000, gets 10% ordinary shares, SHA is signed. Years 1–5 are identical to the original proposal. Daniel receives 10% of all profit distributions.
  2. The ratchet clause is written into the SHA
    One additional clause in the SHA states: “Once cumulative dividend distributions to Daniel Aziz (verified by audited accounts) reach £150,000, DGC Ltd has the right to purchase 5% of Daniel Aziz’s ordinary shares at par value (£75,000) within 90 days.” This is agreed upfront before any money moves.
  3. Year 5 — the payback point and ratchet trigger
    By Year 5, Daniel’s cumulative dividends reach £150,000 — he has received his full investment back through profit distributions alone. The ratchet now triggers. DGC pays Daniel a further £75,000 in cash and cancels 5% of his shares. He now holds 5% of DGC.
  4. In Year 5 alone, DGC pays Daniel £115,000
    That year’s dividend (£40k as 10% of the distribution) plus the £75k buyback payment = £115,000 total leaving DGC in a single year. This is the key cash flow point Ash must plan for — make sure DGC can absorb it.
  5. From Year 6 onwards — Daniel at 5%
    Daniel now owns 5% of DGC permanently. His annual income from dividends is half what it was: around £23k/year in Year 6, rising to £25k/year at plateau. His stake is worth 5% of DGC’s value — approximately £200,000 at the £10M plateau.
  6. DGC buyout option still applies from Year 5
    If DGC wants to buy Daniel out entirely from Year 5, the buyback formula applies to his remaining 5%: 4× profit × 5%. At plateau that’s approximately £200,000 — far less than buying out 10% at £400,000.
Honest pros and cons

WHAT WORKS

  • DGC recovers 5% of the company once the debt has genuinely been repaid. The dilution is directly tied to value received.
  • Daniel still has meaningful upside — permanent 5% in a £4M+ business is a solid long-term position. He’s not cut out, just right-sized.
  • Total cost to DGC over 17 years drops from £1.89M (original) to approximately £525k — a saving of over £1.3M in the optimistic scenario.
  • Incentives are aligned in Phase 1: Daniel wants DGC to grow (because his dividends grow) and wants to hit the £150k payback threshold.
  • Structurally clean — one extra clause in the SHA. Not a fundamentally different deal.

WHAT TO WATCH

  • Year 5 cash hit: DGC must find £115,000 in a single year (£40k dividend + £75k buyback). Plan for this. If cashflow is tight in Year 5, this could be a problem.
  • Daniel may resist. If he’s expecting permanent 10%, the ratchet feels like a clawback. Present it as “built-in flexibility” from the start — not something introduced after he’s agreed to 10%.
  • 5% stays forever — Daniel is still permanently on the cap table. Any future sale still requires his participation.
  • More complex SHA to draft — the ratchet trigger clause needs precise legal language. Estimated extra solicitor cost: £500–£800.
  • At Year 5, Daniel’s exit total dips slightly (£390k to £385k) because the buyback doesn’t fully compensate for the stake drop. A subtle negative worth knowing.

Option C — Profit-share loan

£150,000 as a loan — Daniel gets 15% of annual profits until he’s received £300,000

This deal has no shares and no equity. Daniel lends DGC £150,000. In return, each year when DGC distributes profit to Ash and Harry, Daniel receives 15% of that distribution. This continues until Daniel has received a total of £300,000 — exactly 2× his original investment. Once he hits that £300k figure, the agreement ends completely. No further payments, ever. DGC owns 100% of itself from that point forward.

Grace period: Year 0 and Year 1. No payments are made in either of the first two years. The money goes into the business and DGC uses it. Daniel’s first payment arrives at the Year 2 year-end, once the business has had time to grow with the capital.

Daniel is not on the cap table. He has no shares to sell. He does not participate in a future sale of DGC beyond what he’s already received. He has a contract that pays him well and then ends cleanly.

At current projections, Daniel receives his £300,000 around Year 7. His annualised return is approximately 9% per year — better than almost any savings product, lower than the equity deal’s theoretical upside, but with a clear, guaranteed finish line.

How this deal works — step by step
  1. Daniel writes a cheque for £150,000 — as a loan
    The money goes into DGC exactly as before. The key difference: no shares are issued. Daniel has a loan agreement and a profit-share contract. He does not appear on the company’s shareholder register.
  2. A simple profit-share agreement is signed
    An Isle of Man solicitor drafts a straightforward agreement (approx. £500–£800 — far simpler than a full SHA). It says: “DGC Ltd will pay Daniel Aziz 15% of all distributable profit distributions until cumulative payments reach £300,000, at which point this agreement terminates.”
  3. Year 0 and Year 1 — nothing paid, full grace period
    The money goes into DGC in Year 0 and works in the business for two full years. No payment is made to Daniel in Year 0 or Year 1. The first payment arrives at the Year 2 year-end, once DGC has had time to grow with the capital.
  4. From Year 2 onwards, Daniel receives 15% of whatever Ash and Harry pay themselves
    If Ash and Harry take out £250,000 in Year 2, Daniel gets £37,500. If they take out £300,000 in Year 3, Daniel gets £45,000. His payment is directly tied to DGC’s performance — better year, more for everyone including Daniel.
  5. Year 7 — Daniel hits £300,000 and the agreement ends
    Around Year 7, Daniel’s cumulative payments reach £300,000 — exactly 2× his £150,000 investment. The agreement automatically terminates. DGC makes no further payments, ever. Daniel has no further claim on the business.
  6. From Year 8 onwards — DGC keeps 100% of everything
    Ash and Harry take the full distribution. If DGC is at £10M turnover paying out £500,000 per year, every penny of that goes to Ash and Harry in their 50/50 split. Daniel is done. His agreement is over. He holds no claim on any future profits or any future sale of DGC.
  7. Daniel’s return: £300,000 on £150,000 over 7 years
    That is 2× his money, or approximately 10.4% annualised over 7 years. To put that in context: the FTSE 100 averages 7–8% per year with full market risk. Daniel is getting above-market returns on a family investment with no share price volatility and no risk of his capital going to zero.
Honest pros and cons

WHAT WORKS

  • DGC gives away zero equity. Ash and Harry remain 50/50 owners forever. No permanent dilution, no cap table complication, no stranger in the room on a future sale.
  • Total cost to DGC is fixed at £300,000. No matter how successful DGC becomes, Daniel never receives more than that. The equity deal could cost DGC £1.89M+ over 17 years — this is £1.59M cheaper in the optimistic scenario.
  • Natural endpoint at Year 7. The relationship with Daniel as an “investor” has a clean close. No ongoing obligation, no annual conversations about dividends.
  • Simple agreement — half the legal cost of a full SHA. No valuation needed. No accountant certificate. Quicker to execute.
  • Daniel’s 10.4% annualised return is genuinely competitive. This is not a bad deal for him.

WHAT TO WATCH

  • Daniel has no shares to sell. If DGC grows to £10M and is worth £4M, Daniel has already exited at £300k and sees none of that upside. He may feel this is unfair in retrospect if DGC does extremely well.
  • No equity means Daniel has no “stake in the story.” If his motivation is partly about being part of what Ash is building — not just the return — this deal doesn’t give him that. Must test his real motivation before proposing.
  • If DGC grows faster than projected, Daniel hits his £300k cap sooner (Year 5–6 instead of Year 7) and earns a lower annualised return. Fast growth is better for DGC but reduces Daniel’s effective return.
  • If DGC grows slower, Daniel waits longer for his £300k. He has no shares to sell in the meantime. His capital is tied up for potentially 8–10 years with no liquidity.
  • If presented after Daniel has already been shown the equity deal, he will feel this is a step back. This deal works best when presented first, before any equity expectations are set.

The fixed £300k cost vs the equity deal’s £1.89M+ represents a £1.59M saving in long-term wealth that stays with Ash and Harry. “Founders who preserve equity at the growth stage finish significantly wealthier at exit.”
